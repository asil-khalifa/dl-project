An artificial neural network is a computational model inspired by the brain, built from layers of simple units called neurons. Each neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias and applies a non-linear activation function. The earliest trainable model, the perceptron, was proposed by Frank Rosenblatt in 1958 and could only learn linearly separable patterns.

Deep networks stack many layers so that later layers learn increasingly abstract features. They are trained with gradient descent: the loss between the prediction and the target is computed, and the backpropagation algorithm, popularised by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986, applies the chain rule to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight. Common activation functions include the sigmoid, the hyperbolic tangent and the rectified linear unit, or ReLU, which outputs zero for negative inputs and the input itself otherwise. ReLU is popular because it reduces the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks.

Convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, use small learnable filters that slide across an image, sharing weights across positions. Yann LeCun's LeNet-5, published in 1998, read handwritten digits on bank cheques. In 2012, AlexNet won the ImageNet competition by a large margin and started the deep learning boom in computer vision.

Recurrent neural networks process sequences by feeding their hidden state back into the network at each time step. The long short-term memory network, or LSTM, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997, uses gates to remember information over long sequences. In 2017, Vaswani and colleagues published the Transformer in the paper "Attention Is All You Need". The Transformer replaces recurrence with self-attention, which lets every token attend to every other token in parallel, and it is the basis of modern language models such as BERT, T5 and GPT.

Several techniques help networks generalise. Dropout, proposed by Srivastava and colleagues in 2014, randomly switches off neurons during training so that the network cannot rely on any single unit. Batch normalisation, introduced by Ioffe and Szegedy in 2015, normalises the activations of each layer and allows higher learning rates. The Adam optimiser, proposed by Kingma and Ba in 2014, adapts the learning rate of each parameter using estimates of the first and second moments of the gradients. Transfer learning reuses a network pre-trained on a large dataset and fine-tunes it on a smaller task, which is how most practical deep learning systems are built today.
